# GRISAILLE
### Design Document v0.7

> *Grisaille* (n.) — a painting executed entirely in shades of grey; the monochrome underpainting a master lays down before glazing color over the top.

## 1. Premise

Color was a worked material — mined, ground, traded, guarded by guilds. Then came the Whitening, and it was taken. The world is now the underpainting: everything still there, nothing yet colored.

The player fights to liberate the colors one at a time. Every color recovered permanently returns to the world, adding both visual richness and new gameplay systems.

---

## 2. Narrative

### 2.1 The Whitening

A conservator called **Titanium** — named for the pigment so opaque it covers anything beneath it — concluded that color was the root of every war ever fought. Difference creates division; division creates blood. So he took all of it.

The physics is the thematic engine, and it's real:

> **Light combined makes white. Pigment combined makes grey.**

Gathered as light, the six colors became Titanium, a single blinding white. What settled out as pigment became the grey world. The world and its thief are two halves of the same subtraction.

### 2.2 The Player — Payne

Named for Payne's grey, the pigment mixed from blue, black and a touch of red.

The player is not colorless. They are **everything, compressed and inert** — when Titanium drew the light out, the matter had to settle somewhere, and it settled into one body. Payne is the sediment of the world's color. That is why Payne is the only vessel capable of holding it again.

This resolves the rescue-vs-survival question: it is a **restoration** story. Payne is not a victim of the Whitening, Payne is its residue.

### 2.3 The Wardens

The three primaries are each held by a Warden. Critically: **the Wardens are not villains and not hostages. Each one volunteered.** Each has a genuine argument, and the game should never fully refute any of them.

| Warden | Color | Method | Argument |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Vermilion** | Red | Banked in a cold forge | Red was warmth, appetite, courage — and every war ever started. "I have never slept so well." |
| **Ultramarine** | Blue | Drowned | Blue was depth, distance, grief. Without it no one mourns. Without it no one remembers, either. |
| **Orpiment** | Yellow | Buried | Yellow was light, truth, revelation. It showed people things they had arranged very carefully not to see. |

**Liberation has a cost, and it is a mechanic.** Taking a color back means taking *all* of it. Recovering red doesn't only make the world warmer — red things begin hunting the player. This is the enemy-faction system (§11.1) carrying narrative weight: each faction that enters the pool is the returned shadow of the color the player just chose to bring back.

### 2.4 The Kilns — why secondaries work differently

Green, orange and purple **cannot be liberated**. They were never stolen, because they never existed as separate things — they only ever occurred where two colors touched.

So the secondaries have no Wardens and no Vaults. They have **kilns**. The player doesn't recover them; the player *makes* them.

This is the thematic core and it maps exactly onto the resonance system (§9):

- Titanium's philosophy is that all color reduces to one. Subtraction is the only move he knows.
- Payne's counter-proof is that colors combine into things that never existed before.
- **Synthesis is the one move Titanium cannot answer.**

**Corollary — there is no Green boss, no Orange boss, no Purple boss.** Any roster containing "the Weaver of Green" or similar has deleted the central idea. The secondaries are earned at kilns, not taken from anyone. Guard this in every brief.

The kilns are now a defined encounter structure, not just a theme. See §7.6.

### 2.5 Titanium

The final encounter. He holds every color, so he fights using the player's own build against them (§11.5). He is what Payne becomes by absorbing endlessly and never creating — the logical end of accumulation without synthesis.

**His name is L. Hargreaves.** Every other antagonist in the game is named for a mineral. He is named for nothing — a mundane, clerical surname. *Titanium* is what he became, not who he is.

**He is staff, not management.** His badge reads ARCHIVE STAFF, A-17. He was never the director. He took the world's colors **on his own initiative**, because in his professional judgment it was the correct conservation decision. Nobody authorised it. Nobody stopped him. This is far more unsettling than any version where he holds authority, and it explains why the Wardens *volunteered* rather than obeyed — they agreed with a colleague.

**The condition report is the game's narrative device.** Hargreaves assesses everything on a standard form:

> ACCESSION NO. 88-19-A · OBJECT: Marble Bust (Roman) · CONDITION: Surface soiling — light. Nose — chipped. Left ear — minor loss. Stable. · RECOMMENDATION: Dry clean. Monitor.

That is how he sees a priceless artifact. Uses:

- **Warden introductions** presented as condition reports on each color, in his handwriting.
- **The final encounter opens with him filing one on Payne.** Object: figure, compacted pigment. Condition: cracked throughout. Recommendation: contain.
- **Death screens** as re-shelving paperwork (§7.7).

Cold, procedural, and it characterises him more effectively than any monologue.

**VISUAL LOCK — Titanium is never a monarch.** No throne, no crown, no halo, no sceptre, no robes, no cathedral. He is an elderly archivist in a plain lab coat and cotton conservation gloves, holding a clipboard, standing in a well-lit storage aisle. Mild, tidy, apologetic, entirely certain he did the right thing. Bureaucratic horror, not dark fantasy.

This has drifted twice in concept art, because "final boss" pulls monarch imagery by default. When briefing art, describe him only as an archivist and avoid the words *boss*, *final*, *throne* and *king* entirely.

The one unnatural element: he should be **noticeably more overexposed than his surroundings** — not merely pale, but visibly *the white that covers everything beneath it*. This is the only visual cue that he is more than staff, and it should do all the work that a crown would otherwise do.

### 2.6 The Ending — DECIDED: authored

Titanium falls and the colors are loose, with Payne the only vessel able to hold them. Keep them contained, or release them into a world that will absolutely start using red on itself again within a generation?

**The ending is authored, and it is release.** The entire game is an argument — synthesis beats subtraction — and an argument that stops one sentence short of its conclusion isn't an argument. Offering the containment ending as a player choice would mean the game is agnostic about its own thesis. It isn't.

Hargreaves is never refuted, though. He is right that it will happen. The authored ending must not hide this: somewhere in the recolored epilogue, in the corner of a frame, red is already being used badly. The game's answer is not "he was wrong about the cost." The answer is the kiln — what subtraction can only protect, synthesis makes *more of*, and that is worth the cost. Payne releases the colors knowing the price, which is the only version of the choice that means anything.

Closing image: a final condition report, filled in on the world itself. OBJECT: world. CONDITION: cracked throughout. Colors — fugitive. Unstable. RECOMMENDATION: none.

## 3. Aesthetic Direction

### 3.1 The Conservator Frame

Titanium is **not a painter and not a god of erasure**. He is a conservator. He didn't destroy color, he *preserved* it — put the world into climate-controlled storage for its own protection. Museum-archive logic, not artistic or divine logic.

Everything downstream should follow from this:

- Vaults are **storage facilities**: racking, humidity control, inventory tags, condition reports.
- Wardens are **curators**, not mystics.
- The greyscale world is not burnt or dead. It is *bleached, catalogued and covered in dust sheets*.
- The tone is bureaucratic preservation. Cold, orderly, well-intentioned. Nobody here is cackling.

This is also the primary differentiator from *Clair Obscur: Expedition 33*, which shares vocabulary (chiaroscuro/grisaille, painting-as-cosmology, a single figure removing things from the world). The games diverge completely below the surface — theirs is a turn-based RPG about inherited grief, this is a roguelite arguing that synthesis beats subtraction — but the shelf-glance similarity is real and should be actively pushed against. Conservator-not-painter is the cheapest and strongest lever.

### 3.2 The Guilds Are Industrial and Toxic

Second differentiator: where *Expedition 33* is ethereal and Belle Époque, **Grisaille is grubby, material and industrial.** Real pigment history is filthy and violent, and it does the design work for free:

| Pigment | Reality | Guild character |
|---|---|---|
| **Vermilion** | Ground cinnabar — mercury ore. Poisons the people who make it. | A mercury smelter. Jagged crimson crystal shedding beads of liquid metal that roll and pool. Fast, hot, aggressive. |
| **Ultramarine** | Lapis from a single Afghan valley. Once worth more than gold by weight. | A flooded mine at the end of a trade route people died on. Layered sedimentary strata. Slow, heavy, drowning. |
| **Orpiment** | Arsenic sulfide. An alarming acid yellow that looks like it wants to hurt you. | Radial, spiny, luminous. Snipes from range and lights the player up for everything else. |
| **Scheele's Green** | Arsenic. It was in Victorian wallpaper, and it killed people in their own homes. | Not a body at all — a *damask motif* that creeps and spreads across floors and walls. |
| **Realgar (Orange)** | Orpiment's unstable sibling. Degrades in light. | Bursts. |
| **Tyrian Purple** | Murex sea snails, ~10,000 per gram. Vats so foul the dyeworks were banished outside city walls. | Spirals, shells, rot. Phases in and out. |

This reframes §2.3 usefully: the Wardens didn't renounce color out of philosophy alone. They renounced it out of **guilt**, because they ran the supply chains and know exactly what production cost in bodies.

### 3.3 The Palette Inversion

**The greyscale world is light, not dark.** Bone, chalk, plaster, paper, dust sheets. Museum storage is pale.

**Payne is the darkest thing on screen. Always.** A near-black blot moving across a pale world is instantly findable in any crowd, at any zoom, permanently. This solves top-down legibility on day one and is thematically exact — Payne is sediment, the compacted residue of everything drawn out of the world.

### 3.4 Payne, and Cracks as Diegetic UI

Payne's body is compacted pigment: dense, dry, fissured like cracked clay.

Liberated colors do not appear as armor or costume. They **bleed up through the fissures from inside**, because they were always in there. The top-down camera mostly sees back and shoulders, so that is where the crack network lives.

Then make it functional:

> **Glow intensity in each fissure IS that color's charge meter (§8.6).**

Fire red until spent and the red seams go dark and dormant. No HUD element, no bars. The player reads their complete resource state off their own back. By endgame Payne is a black silhouette webbed with six pulsing veins of light, dimming and reigniting as they rotate.

### 3.5 Enemy Tiers

**Tier 1 — The Conservation Staff (grey, from the Prologue).** Not monsters. *Apparatus.* Machined right angles, orderly patrol patterns, entirely procedural. They are not angry at the player — they are *processing* the player. Everything reads as clerical.

| Name | Form | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| **Clamper** | Articulated frame clamp | Grapples, holds the player in place |
| **Framekeeper** | Walking stretcher frame | Blocks lanes, deploys cover |
| **Wash Drifter** | Floating dust sheet | Drifts like a manta ray, obscures vision |
| **Vacuum Scribe** | Suction table with a pen arm | Pulls the player, marks the floor |
| **Rack Warden** | Mobile shelving unit | Reconfigures the room's geometry |
| **Plasterling** | Small chalk swarmer | Cheap, numerous, chip damage |
| **Linen Husk** | Sheeted humanoid shroud | Slow, high HP, absorbs a full magazine |

**Tier 2 — The Guilds (released per liberated color).** Material and industrial, per the table in §3.2. Each enters the shared pool permanently and resists its own color (§11.1).

### 3.6 Pigment Staining

**Colored enemies permanently stain the floor where they die.**

Every kill deposits a pigment smear. Clear a room and the arena becomes a painting — the player's painting, a record of exactly how they fought. A purple singularity that knots a crowd together leaves one dense bloom where they all died.

Implementation is decals, so it is cheap. The payoff is that the core promise is delivered *continuously* rather than only at boss kills. The world doesn't regain color in cutscenes; the player smears it back on by hand.

**Death stains too.** When Payne is re-shelved (§7.7), a permanent dark mark is left where it happened. The hub and the Vaults become a record of failures as well as clears.

**RESOLVED — permanence vs. readability: stains dry.** Permanence is the promise and it stays. Instead of fading out, stains **dry**:

- A fresh kill lands at full saturation for a beat — the visceral payoff — then settles within seconds to a hard matte ceiling.
- Over subsequent visits, stains dry further toward dusty chalk washes, like watercolour left in the sun. They **never disappear**. A hundred-run room is thick with pale history, not blank.
- Live entities and projectiles are **always more saturated and higher contrast** than any floor stain. Non-negotiable.
- Stains render on a separate pass with a **hard saturation and value ceiling**, so the constraint can never regress by accident.

**RESOLVED — hub staining: yes.** Every homecoming, every death mark, every tracked-in smear persists in the hub. A veteran's hub is a save file you can look at. This is cheap and it is the game's own thesis rendered in decals.

### 3.7 Production Notes

- **Flat geometric shapes** plus a **single canvas-grain overlay across the entire screen.** That one shader unifies everything, disguises the simplicity of the underlying assets, and sells the painting conceit for free.
- **Silhouette does all the work** — which §11.2's shape signatures already require nailing regardless.
- Minimal animation requirements. Apparatus and mineral forms need no character rigs.

## 4. The Core Insight

**Color is information — and liberation buys the *speed* of reading it, not the possibility.**

v0.6 stated this as "the player genuinely cannot read the game," which quietly contradicted the accessibility commitment in §11.2 (shape and motion signatures from day one). If shapes carry the information fully, color adds nothing; if they don't, colorblind players are locked out of the core mechanic. Resolved with a perception hierarchy that is real cognitive science:

- **Shape and motion signatures are always legible — but attentive.** They must be foveated and parsed one entity at a time, serially. In a crowd, that costs everything.
- **Color is preattentive.** It is parsed in parallel across the entire screen, instantly, for free.

So before liberating red, red-aligned entities render in greyscale *with their jagged signature intact*. The player **can** read them — by staring at each one individually, while twelve others are shooting. After liberation, the same crowd resolves at a glance.

Liberating a color upgrades perception from *possible* to *effortless*. Progression = perception bandwidth. This is honest to colorblind players — the attentive tier is never taken away, and it is what they were always going to lean on — and it makes the flip moment *stronger*, because the player has just spent twenty minutes feeling exactly what serial reading costs.

The flip test in §13 is restated to match.

## 5. Scope & Genre

- **Scope:** solo developer, long-form ambitious project.
- **Color arc:** persistent across the save file, not per-run.
- **Combat:** top-down twin-stick shooter (reference: *Enter the Gungeon*, *Nuclear Throne*).
- **Structure:** roguelite with a persistent hub (reference: *Hades*).

Twin-stick preserves the bullet-pattern reading of a classic shmup, scales up to survivors-style crowds when desired, and is tractable solo — it needs no complex character animation.

---

## 6. Core Loop

1. Greyscale hub.
2. Raid a Color Vault, or fire a Kiln (procedurally assembled run).
3. Defeat the Warden, or complete the Firing.
4. The color is **permanently** added — hub, item pool, enemy pool, and all future runs gain that color's palette, verbs, and hostile faction.
5. Return to hub, now measurably more colorful. Repeat.

## 7. Run Structure

### 7.1 The problem: permanent weapons kill run variance

In *Gungeon* or *Hades*, **weapon acquisition is the run**. That is the entire variance engine. Grisaille has spent it on meta-progression — once six colors are owned, all 36 combinations are owned, permanently.

So a run must generate variance from somewhere else, or every late-game raid feels identical. Three systems do it, and they are load-bearing.

### 7.2 Pigment as the run economy

The player enters a Vault with **limited reserves of each owned color**. Firing drains them (§8.6). Killing an enemy drops *its* pigment — so fighting red things is how red gets replenished.

The consequence is that ammo is color-specific and **directional**. Dry on blue means going where blue lives. Routing becomes resource planning rather than pure risk assessment, and the player's build is constantly pressured by what the map is actually offering.

**The cross-harvest loop — this is a first-class design, not an emergent accident.** Because every faction resists its own color (§11.1), the efficient way to farm red is to kill red things *with blue*. The player **spends the color they have to harvest the color they lack**, then rotates. The resistance system and the ammo economy are one system, and together they generate the routing decision, the swap pressure (§7.4), and the build rotation (§11.3) simultaneously. If either half is ever tuned in isolation, this loop is what breaks first — tune them as a pair.

### 7.3 Swatches as the per-run build

Payne is robbing an archive, so the pickups are **loose pigment samples** that buff *one slot of one color*:

- "Red delivery pierces."
- "Blue payload chills 40% faster."
- "Yellow delivery no longer overheats."

Swatches **stack onto the permanent grid rather than replacing it**. Today the blue lob is enormous, so the run is blue-delivery with rotating payloads; tomorrow it is chain lightning and the identical arsenal plays completely differently.

Second function: because each swatch pushes toward particular combinations, it also steers which **resonance** is accumulating (§9). The run's random build therefore partly decides which kiln unlocks next — run variance feeding meta-progression instead of sitting beside it.

**Fugitive vs permanent.** Real pigments are graded by how they survive light exposure. *Fugitive* swatches are strictly stronger but fade as the run progresses; *permanent* ones are weaker and last. Free risk/reward axis, historically accurate, thematically on-message.

### 7.4 Moment-to-moment: hot-swapping under pressure

Every faction resists its own color (§11.1), so a **mixed room is the core of the game**. Red chargers shrug off burn, blue drowners shrug off chill, and they occupy the same arena.

The player dodges bullet patterns while reconfiguring delivery and payload every few seconds to match whatever is currently threatening them. Rock-paper-scissors executed at twitch speed on a two-slot grid.

**This is the game's fingerprint**, and it is the reason the swap input must be physical and mid-combat rather than a menu (§11.4).

### 7.5 Raid structure — Vaults

A Vault is a storage facility. Layout follows from that:

- **Branching route map**, node icons showing which guild occupies each bay — so the player can see where their needed pigment is.
- **Three wings** converging on the Warden.
- Target length: **25–35 minutes**.

| Room type | Function |
|---|---|
| **Processing floors** | Open arenas. Crowds, mixed factions, the swapping showcase. |
| **Racking aisles** | Corridors. Cover, sightlines, precision shooting. |
| **Archives** | Non-combat. Swatch selection, lore, condition reports. |
| **Vault core** | Warden encounter. |

### 7.6 Kiln runs — the Firing *(new)*

The kilns were the thematic heart of the game with no verb attached. This section gives Act III its challenge structure.

**Where.** Kilns are not in the archive. They sit in the **unaccessioned territory** — the ruined guild districts outside climate control, never catalogued because Hargreaves judged them not worth preserving. A kiln run travels *outward* through industrial ruin instead of inward through storage: grinding floors, dye vats, drying racks, collapsed workshop halls. Same procedural room grammar, opposite texture — dereliction instead of order. Resonance (§9) reveals a kiln's location; the route to it is a run of normal length.

**The Firing — the capstone encounter that replaces a boss.** The player lights the kiln and must hold it at temperature through the full firing. The rules:

1. **Only kills made with the target mix feed the kiln.** For the Purple Kiln, that means any weapon carrying one red slot and one blue slot, in either arrangement. Off-mix and mono-color kills feed nothing.
2. **Both parent factions attack in waves — and each resists half of the mandated mix.** The red guild shrugs off the burn half; the blue guild shrugs off the chill half. The player fights with a deliberately blunted instrument against enemies built to blunt it.
3. **The temptation to swap off-mix IS the fight.** At every single moment, switching to the pure counter-color is locally optimal — and feeds the kiln nothing, and lets it cool. The Firing is an endurance test of exactly one thing: *sustaining synthesis when subtraction is always the easier move.* The player must physically refuse Titanium's logic under fire for the full duration. That is the whole thesis as an input pattern.
4. **The economy closes on itself.** Firing the mix drains both parent meters simultaneously (§8.6), and the attacking parent factions are the only refill source. The player harvests the assault to sustain the assault.
5. **The final phase: liberation-has-a-cost, delivered in the same minute as the reward.** As the kiln reaches temperature, the new color's faction is *born from it* — Scheele's Green damask creeps out of the kiln's mouth as the first green thing that has ever existed, and it is immediately hostile. It resists itself, forcing the player back onto the parents' pure colors for the last stand. The first thing the player's creation does is turn on them, exactly as §2.3 promises.

**Nobody is defeated.** No Warden, no argument, no condition report filed on a person. Nothing is taken from anyone. The kiln simply fires, and the world contains a color it never contained before. This is the one victory in the game Hargreaves has no paperwork for.

**Failure:** the kiln cools, and the Conservation Staff retrieve Payne from the field — retrieval is also procedure, and the re-shelving form has a checkbox for "recovered off-site." Standard death rules (§7.7) apply.

### 7.7 Death is re-shelving

Payne is sediment and cannot meaningfully be killed. On defeat, the **Conservation Staff simply collect the player and return them to storage.**

- **Kept:** all liberated colors, all permanent progress.
- **Lost:** carried pigment **above Working Stock** (below).
- This makes **hauling capacity a live decision** late in a run — push one more bay, or bank what is already held. It also keeps the antagonists clerical rather than murderous (§3.1), which is the tone the whole game is built on.

**RESOLVED — the death spiral: Working Stock.** In v0.6, pigment was both ammo and the death penalty, so a deep death started the next run dry — the losing player got less to fight with, a compounding failure loop. Fix:

> **Re-shelving refills every owned color to a baseline Working Stock.** Losses only bite above the floor.

The diegetic justification is a gift: the re-shelving form has a field for standard-issue working stock, so the staff **issue it automatically, because procedure**. Payne is re-shelved *with* supplies by the antagonists' own bureaucracy — Hargreaves's meticulousness is the player's safety net, and he would never notice, because the form is correct. Working Stock is upgradeable in the hub via **Requisition Forms** (filed paperwork as a progression currency, consistent with §7.9's stationery economy).

### 7.8 The Prologue problem *(restructured — was "the Act I problem")*

With only red, none of the systems above exist: no swapping, no resistance puzzles, no mixing, one weapon. v0.6 flagged this and said "keep Act I short." That undersold the risk — the one-color stretch is the demo, the first hour, and the refund window, competing head-on with *Gungeon* using a fraction of the toolkit.

**RESOLVED — blue is front-loaded. The one-color stretch is now a Prologue, hard-capped at ~20 minutes.**

- **Prologue — The Inventory.** Greyscale, Conservation Staff only, pure dodge-and-read execution. A small, tight precision shmup that exists to make the player *feel* the cost of attentive-tier reading (§4). Ends at Vermilion's cold forge. Red is recovered inside the first half hour.
- **Ultramarine's Vault is open immediately.** No gate, no second hub loop of grinding. A focused player has blue — and therefore mixing, the swap input, resistances, and the cross-harvest loop — inside the first session. The grid hits 4 while the first impression is still forming.

The Prologue stays in the prototype list (§13) as the second thing built, because even at 20 minutes it is still the most likely bounce point.

### 7.9 Relics & Archive Items

A second pickup class alongside swatches (§7.3): **archive bureaucracy as an item economy.** Passive relics found in Archives and carried for the rest of the run.

| Relic | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Pigment Vial** | +damage for one color |
| **Catalogue Leaf** | +knowledge — reveals unexplored bay contents on the route map |
| **Color Lock** | +slot — a second swatch may occupy one delivery or payload |
| **Humidity Gauge** | +control — slows fugitive swatch decay (§7.3) |
| **Crate Seal** | +luck — improves swatch rarity in Archives |
| **Conservator's Key** | +access — opens sealed bays and shortcut routes |
| **Swatch Book** | +mix — resonance gain multiplier (§9) |
| **Restoration Permit** | +reroll — reroll an Archive's swatch offering |

Two of these are doing structural work rather than just stat work. **Conservator's Key** turns the branching route map into something the player can partially unlock mid-run. **Swatch Book** lets a player deliberately chase a specific kiln, which converts resonance from a passive drip into a build the player can commit to.

The tone matters as much as the effects: every relic is a piece of stationery, paperwork or lab equipment. Nothing is a sword or an amulet. The player is robbing a records office.

## 8. The Weapon System

### 8.1 The problem being solved

Hand-authoring a unique weapon per color pair does not scale. Six colors = 36 combinations; authoring each individually kills the project. **Combos must be generated, not authored.**

### 8.2 Two slots

Every weapon is composed of two independently-colored slots:

- **Delivery** — how the projectile leaves the gun.
- **Payload** — what happens on contact.

Any color can fill either slot. `n` colors yields `n²` weapons from only `2n` authored pieces.

### 8.3 Deliveries

Each delivery is named for a painter's tool. This is the player-facing vocabulary.

| Color | Name | Delivery | Character |
|---|---|---|---|
| Red | **Brush** | Rapid stream | High fire rate, short range, sprays |
| Blue | **Roller** | Arcing lob | Clears cover, slow, heavy |
| Yellow | **Quill** | Hitscan beam | Instant, pierces, overheats fast |
| Green | **Sponge** | Seeking spores | Homing, slow, persistent |
| Orange | **Bellows** | Cluster burst | Shotgun spread of fragments |
| Purple | **Compass** | Phase shot | Passes through walls and bodies, hits a line |

Weapons are therefore spoken as *"Brush of Chill"*, *"Compass of Detonate"* — tool plus effect. Six tool names cover all 36 combinations.

**Period-correctness rule.** Every delivery must be a tool that could plausibly exist in a pigment guild's workshop. *Sprayer* (aerosol, 20th century) and *Palette* (a surface, not a verb) were both rejected on these grounds. Alternates if needed: **Muller** — the glass tool used to grind pigment on a slab — reads as grinding rather than bursting, so it is a weaker fit for orange.

### 8.4 Payloads

| Color | Payload | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Red | Burn | DoT, spreads to adjacent enemies |
| Blue | Chill | Stacks to freeze, then shatter |
| Yellow | Chain | Arcs to nearby targets |
| Green | Leech | Heals player, leaves creeping hazard patch |
| Orange | Detonate | Area blast, knockback |
| Purple | Singularity | Pulls enemies toward impact point |

### 8.5 Notable combinations

- **Blue lob + Orange detonate** — the classic grenade.
- **Purple phase + Yellow chain** — pierce a wall, light up the room behind it.
- **Purple singularity + Orange detonate** — vacuum the crowd into a ball, then delete it. The signature combo.
- **Green seeking + Red burn** — homing napalm.
- **Yellow beam + Green leech** — hitscan lifesteal.
- **Red stream + Blue chill** — sustained freeze pressure on a single target.

### 8.6 The diagonal (mono-color mastery)

Matching both slots yields the pure, oversaturated color with a unique tier-3 effect. These should be the **highest raw damage in the game**.

Self-balancing mechanism: firing drains the charge meter of every color in use. A mixed weapon splits that drain across two meters; a mono-color weapon dumps it all into one.

> **Mono-color = burst. Mixed = sustain.**

Mastery builds become a genuine strategic choice rather than a trap or a crutch, and never need nerfing. Narratively, mono-color purity is also Titanium's logic — the player who refuses to mix is playing the antagonist's game. The Firing (§7.6) makes this literal: a kiln cannot be fed by a purist.

## 9. Resonance (Unlock Gating)

**The mix is the key, not the reward.**

If red + blue already fires purple projectiles, "liberating" purple risks feeling redundant. Inverted:

- Landing kills with purple-tinted weapons (any red/blue mix) builds **purple resonance**.
- Resonance reveals the location of the Purple Kiln in the unaccessioned territory (§7.6).
- Combos are therefore not a side system — they are how the map is unlocked.

A player who only ever fires red will physically run out of game. This forces experimentation without a tutorial telling them to experiment, and it is the mechanical expression of §2.4. The Firing then examines the same skill resonance trained: the reveal is earned by mixing casually, the kiln is earned by mixing under duress.

## 11. Design Problems to Solve Early

### 11.1 Difficulty inverts
Every liberated color makes the game *more readable*, so the curve runs backwards by default.

**Fix:** each liberated color introduces its own enemy faction into the shared pool — which §2.3 already justifies narratively. Factions resist their own color (the Red faction shrugs off burn), forcing build rotation and powering the cross-harvest economy (§7.2).

### 11.2 Accessibility
Roughly 8% of men have some form of color vision deficiency. The perception hierarchy in §4 is the resolution: **the attentive tier — shape and motion signatures — exists from day one and is never removed.** Red is jagged, blue is flowing, yellow is radiant.

For CVD players, liberation still delivers a real upgrade — luminance, saturation and value contrast are preattentive channels too, so palettes must be authored with CVD-safe value separation, not hue alone (red/green pairs especially). What changes is the *size* of the upgrade, not its existence, and the shape tier keeps the game fully playable throughout. Retrofitting signatures later would be miserable, and the shape language makes the greyscale Prologue read significantly better regardless.

### 11.3 Build stagnation
Players will find one dominant pair and never rotate off it.

**Fix:** the per-color charge meter (§8.6) forces rotation every few seconds. Faction resistances (§11.1) and the cross-harvest economy (§7.2) reinforce it. The Firing (§7.6) periodically *examines* it.

### 11.4 Combo input must be physical
Swapping colors belongs in combat, not in a pause menu. Target a held-modifier-plus-tap input executable mid-fight.

**References:** *Magicka* (live element queuing), *Noita* (depth of wand composition), *Ikaruga* (polarity switching under pressure).

### 11.5 The Titanium mirror
The final boss reading and replaying the player's own build is the hardest single piece of engineering in the game. Prototype it as a spike early enough to cut without grief. **The cut line is pre-authored:** if the mirror doesn't work, the fight ships as a hand-authored rotation through all six colors — still thematically correct (he holds everything), just less personal. Nothing else in the game depends on the mirror, so this is the designated scope release valve.

## 12. Technical Pipeline — The Make-or-Break

**If VFX and audio don't compose procedurally, the 36-weapon savings evaporate and the project dies.**

- **VFX:** one particle system. A projectile takes a *delivery emitter* and a *payload tint*, blended at runtime. Red emitter + blue payload renders purple automatically — this is just color math, and correct color theory doubles as the UI.
- **Audio:** one layer per delivery, one per payload, mixed at runtime.
- **Stains:** decal pass with a hard saturation/value ceiling (§3.6), separate from entity rendering, so the readability constraint is structural rather than disciplinary.

Get this right early and 36 weapons costs barely more than 12.

## 13. Prototype Order

Build in this order; each gate must pass before the next begins.

1. **The flip test** *(restated per §4)*. One room. Greyscale. A **crowd** of red-faction enemies rendered in grey with shape signatures intact — readable, but only one at a time, while all of them shoot. Let the player suffer serial perception for two minutes. Then flip red on: the same crowd resolves at a glance. The chills moment is no longer "I can see" — it is "I can see *everything at once*." If that doesn't land, the premise needs rethinking. If it does, the game and the trailer are both validated. An afternoon of work.
2. **The Prologue slice.** Twenty minutes of one-weapon greyscale execution against Conservation Staff. This is the bounce point (§7.8); it has to be good enough to survive on dodge-and-read alone.
3. **The swap input.** Two colors, one mixed room, the held-modifier-plus-tap grid (§11.4). The game's fingerprint lives or dies on how this feels at twitch speed.
4. **The Firing, grayboxed** *(new)*. One kiln, two parent factions, the feed-only-on-mix rule (§7.6). This must be prototyped before Act III production art, because it is currently the newest design in the document and the only one with no genre precedent to lean on.
5. **The Titanium mirror spike** — with the hand-authored rotation as the pre-committed fallback (§11.5).
